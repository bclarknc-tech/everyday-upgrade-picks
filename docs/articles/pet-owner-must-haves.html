A few pieces of gear solve most of the daily friction of living with a pet — feeding, walking, play, shedding, and sleep. Here's what to look for in each, so you buy the version that actually holds up.

Automatic Pet Feeder

Look for programmable portion control (down to the gram or tablespoon) rather than just timed dispensing, since overfeeding is the biggest risk with these. A hopper with an airtight seal and a locking lid matters more than capacity — it keeps food fresh and keeps determined pets out. Also check that the power supply has a battery backup, because a feeder that stops during an outage defeats the whole point.

No-Pull Dog Harness

The key is a front-clip (chest) attachment point, which redirects the dog's momentum and stops pulling by turning them toward you, rather than a back-clip that actually encourages sledding. Fit is what makes or breaks it: look for adjustable straps at the neck and chest with a Y-shaped front that doesn't sit across the shoulder joints or restrict movement. Padding on the chest strap and a sturdy handle help with control, but avoid anything that tightens or squeezes as a correction.

Interactive Cat Toy

Cats need the toy to move unpredictably on its own, so prioritize erratic, multi-speed motion over simple spin — predictable motion gets ignored within days. Consider whether it has a rechargeable battery and auto-shutoff timer, since these get left running constantly. A toy that lets you swap attachments or change the movement pattern will hold interest far longer than a single fixed action.

Pet Hair Vacuum Attachment

Look for a tool with rubber or silicone "teeth" or a self-cleaning brushroll, since these pull embedded fur out of upholstery and carpet without the hair wrapping around the bristles. A wide, angled head and a swivel joint make it usable on stairs, car seats, and awkward corners where most hair actually hides. Confirm it fits your vacuum's hose diameter, and that the bristles are soft enough for fabric but firm enough for carpet.

Orthopedic Dog Bed

What matters is a dense, high-resilience foam base (memory foam or a firm orthopedic slab) thick enough that your dog's hips and elbows don't sink through to the floor. Look for a removable, machine-washable cover with a waterproof liner, since older dogs have accidents and a bed you can't clean is a bed you'll throw away. Match the size to your dog's full stretched-out length, not their curled-up size, and choose a low entry height if they're arthritic.
